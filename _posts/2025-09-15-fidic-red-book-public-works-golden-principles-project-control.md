On paper, the Engineer had 84 days to agree or determine each claim. On a public water treatment plant, more than a year passed without a single determination, while claims for late site access and a disputed variation piled up "pending the employer's position". Then the contractor pointed out in writing that the time limits under Sub-Clause 3.7 had long expired, and the supervision consultant issued a batch of determinations in a few weeks. Assembled in a hurry, some were challenged within days; others became final and binding, although the employer would have contested them had anyone read them carefully.

The contract said "FIDIC Conditions of Contract for Construction, 2017". The form was not the problem. What failed were the amendments made to it and the way its time limits were run. We see versions of the same story on roads, ports, hospitals and power lines, in Tunisia, in West and Central Africa and on bank-financed projects elsewhere, and the causes are nearly always the same few.

## How a FIDIC contract loses its mechanics

On a road rehabilitation contract, the General Conditions were attached in full, followed by several dozen pages of Particular Conditions written by the employer's legal department. One amendment required the Engineer to obtain the employer's written approval before any decision with a financial effect. Another kept the payment period but made each payment "subject to budget availability". A third replaced the dispute board with a referral to a ministry. Each had an internal reason. Together they left a contract with FIDIC's name and few of its working parts.

FIDIC expects its forms to be adapted: the Contract Data fix the numbers, and the Particular Conditions add what the project and the law require. In 2019 it published five **Golden Principles** for any adaptation. Roles and duties stay as the General Conditions describe them. Amendments are clearly drafted. The balance of risk and reward is not overturned. Time limits stay reasonable. Disputes go to a dispute board before arbitration, unless the governing law prevents it.

Public procurement law imposes real adaptations, and these are legitimate: rules on performance securities, approval of amendments by control commissions, tax and customs provisions, language and governing law. In Tunisia, the 2014 public procurement decree requires a written amendment, after the opinion of the competent control commission, once variations exceed the limit stated in the contract (20 % if the contract is silent). Similar rules exist across the region.

The amendments that cause damage are the ones the law does not require. Four come back most often:

| Amendment | What it breaks |
|---|---|
| Employer's prior approval of determinations | The Engineer can no longer determine within 42 days; claims are deemed rejected and become disputes |
| Payment "subject to budget availability" | The payment periods of Clause 14 lose their meaning, and with them the contractor's cash flow |
| Shortened notice periods for the contractor only | Claims are lost on time bars rather than decided on merit, which invites challenge |
| Dispute board removed or replaced by an administrative referral | No independent, binding step remains before arbitration or the courts |

The Red Book provides the right tool for an employer's internal approval rules. Sub-Clause 3.2 lets the Particular Conditions list specific powers for which the Engineer needs the employer's consent, such as instructing variations above a stated value, but it expressly excludes agreements and determinations under Sub-Clause 3.7. A short approval period for large variations fits public finance rules. A veto over determinations removes the mechanism the rest of the contract relies on.

When we review Particular Conditions for an employer, each amendment has to answer three questions: does the law or the lender require it, what problem does it solve, and which other sub-clauses does it affect? On a port extension, this exercise cut the Particular Conditions to a quarter of their length, and the lender's no-objection came back in one round instead of three.

## Three clocks that run in parallel

The 2017 edition puts most contract administration on fixed time limits, with a deemed outcome when nothing happens. Three clocks matter most during the works, and they interact.

### The claim clock (Sub-Clause 20.2)

A party that considers itself entitled to additional time or money gives a Notice of Claim within 28 days of becoming aware, or when it should have become aware, of the event. A fully detailed claim follows within 84 days. A late Notice can cost the claim, subject to the Engineer's review of the reasons for the delay. On a transmission line, a contractor lost most of a well-founded claim for late right-of-way because its site team had reported the obstruction in progress meetings for weeks without ever sending a Notice; minutes of a meeting are not a Notice under Clause 20. Contractors usually know this clock. Engineers often do not realise that the next one starts from it.

### The determination clock (Sub-Clause 3.7)

When the parties disagree, the Engineer first consults them to reach agreement, then makes a **fair determination**, acting neutrally between them and not as the employer's agent.

| Step | Time limit | If nothing happens |
|---|---|---|
| Consultation to agreement | 42 days from the start date for the matter (for a claim: receipt of the fully detailed claim) | No agreement is deemed reached |
| Determination | 42 days more | A claim is deemed rejected; any other matter becomes a Dispute |
| Clerical errors | Reported within 14 days, corrected within 7 | |
| Notice of Dissatisfaction | 28 days after receiving the determination | The determination becomes final and binding |

![Sub-Clause 3.7 clocks](/assets/blog/fidic-3-7-clocks.svg)
*Figure 2. The 3.7 clocks and their deemed outcomes.*

A determination binds both parties as soon as it is given, and an amount agreed or determined goes into the next payment certificate. The parties comply with it unless and until it is revised.

The failures we see are organisational. The supervision consultant is staffed for inspection, with no planner and no quantity surveyor for claims. Its next contract depends on the employer, which makes neutrality uncomfortable. The determination file is opened when the fully detailed claim arrives, by which time half the facts are hard to establish. For Engineer teams, we open the file on the day the Notice of Claim is received, keep the contemporary records in it from then on, and write each determination like a short judgement: issue, positions of the parties, contractual basis, facts found, analysis, outcome and the time limit for a Notice of Dissatisfaction. A determination written this way survives review by a board. On a hospital project, a determination of two pages that stated the facts found and the sub-clause relied on was accepted by both parties without a Notice of Dissatisfaction; the same Engineer's one-paragraph rejection of an earlier claim went straight to the board.

### The programme clock (Sub-Clauses 8.3, 4.20 and 8.4)

On a pumping station contract, the contractor's planner and the Engineer's planner each brought a bar chart to the monthly meeting. They differed by five months on completion. The initial programme had been rejected eight months earlier with comments the contractor considered unfounded, and nobody had followed up. When the first extension of time claim arrived, there was no accepted baseline to measure it against.

Sub-Clause 8.3 requires an initial programme within 28 days of the notice of commencement, with logic links, float, the critical path, review periods for Contractor's Documents and the timing of inspections and tests. If the Engineer gives no notice within 21 days that the programme does not comply, it becomes *the Programme*. Revised programmes are treated the same way, with a 14-day period. Sub-Clause 4.20 requires a monthly progress report within 7 days of the end of each month, comparing actual and planned progress. Sub-Clause 8.4 obliges both parties and the Engineer to give **advance warning** of events that may affect cost, time or performance.

An extension of time is assessed against the accepted programme, using the method the parties agreed or, failing that, a recognised one such as those described in the Society of Construction Law's *Delay and Disruption Protocol*. Without an accepted programme, every claim becomes a debate about which baseline to use before it becomes a debate about the delay itself. Our practice on the Engineer's side: review the initial programme against the requirements of 8.3 point by point, never by general rejection; answer each monthly report in writing; and keep a short early warning register reviewed at every progress meeting. None of these replaces a Notice of Claim, and the 28-day clock still applies. Together they give a dated written record against which each claim can be checked.

## The DAAB: a board that knows the project

The 2017 edition replaced ad hoc dispute boards with a **standing Dispute Avoidance/Adjudication Board**, appointed within 28 days of the Letter of Acceptance unless the Contract Data state otherwise, with fees shared equally. Under its procedural rules, it visits the site at intervals of between 70 and 140 days. At the joint request of the parties, it can give **informal assistance** on an issue before it becomes a dispute; neither the parties nor the board are bound by that view. If a dispute arises, referral follows within 42 days of a Notice of Dissatisfaction, the board gives its decision within 84 days, and the decision is binding unless and until revised.

![DAAB two tracks](/assets/blog/fidic-daab-two-tracks.svg)
*Figure 3. Avoidance first, adjudication if needed.*

Public employers often treat the board as a cost to cut. Visits are cancelled once members are appointed. On a highway interchange, three disputes were then referred together to members who had seen the site once, two years earlier; the hearings started with a week of site familiarisation at the parties' expense. A board that has visited regularly can decide a referral on facts it has already seen. On a water supply scheme, a single informal opinion from the board on the measurement of rock excavation, given during a routine visit, settled a question that would otherwise have grown into a claim over the following year.

Three things make the board work. Its members are listed in the Contract Data, or a selection process is ready, before the tender is published. Its fees are in the employer's budget from the start. And the claims register and the early warning register are on the agenda of every visit.

## Before the next FIDIC tender

In each of these cases, most of the damage was done before signature. The table below is the check we run with employers at tender stage.

| Mechanism | To settle before tender | To run during the works |
|---|---|---|
| Particular Conditions | Each amendment justified by law, lender or project need; Golden Principles checked | Amendments applied as written, without internal add-ons |
| Engineer (3.2, 3.7) | Powers requiring employer consent listed; determinations excluded; claims staff in the supervision budget | File opened at each Notice of Claim; determinations issued within 42 days, with reasons |
| Programme (8.3, 4.20, 8.4) | Programme requirements and delay analysis method in the Specification | Initial programme reviewed point by point; monthly reports answered; early warnings logged |
| DAAB (Clause 21) | Members or selection process ready; fees budgeted | Visits kept; registers on every agenda; informal assistance used early |

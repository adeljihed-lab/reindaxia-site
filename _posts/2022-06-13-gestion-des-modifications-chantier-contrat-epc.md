On most EPC projects we see, the large changes are handled reasonably well. A new tie-in, a resized vessel or an added pump goes through management of change because everybody can see it is significant. The changes that hurt at mechanical completion are the small ones: a pipe rerouted around a cable tray, a support moved, an instrument turned to face the walkway. Each one is sensible and quick, and is settled verbally between a supervisor and a subcontractor. Months later, hundreds of them stand between the plant and its acceptance certificates.

The fix is a decision route scaled to the impact of each change, a single trace for every decision, and a check at mechanical completion that the plant matches its documents.

## Three weeks for a pipe that moved

During a mechanical completion walkdown on a piping subsystem, the line no longer ran where the isometric said. At one rack it climbed over a cable tray, with two extra elbows and a high point without a vent. The supervisor remembered a clash with the electrical installation, sorted out on the spot. There was no technical query and no marked-up copy. Engineering had to check the line's flexibility, a vent was added, the isometric and the test pack were reworked: three weeks of delay and a contractual discussion for a decision that would have taken ten minutes to write down.

## Why AFC drawings never survive construction

Design, procurement and construction overlap on an EPC project by design. Documents are issued Approved for Construction (AFC) while some vendor data is still moving. The design change procedure of a gas processing project listed the usual sources: vendor information received late, recommendations from the independent verification body or a third party, owner comments, constraints discovered during erection.

None of this is abnormal. What matters is what an uncontrolled change does downstream:

- a routing change can alter a line's flexibility, a drain point, a separation distance, access to a valve or the classification of an area;
- mechanical completion is verified against documents, so an installation that does not match them cannot be accepted;
- the operator will work for decades from the as-built records, and every error in them becomes a badly prepared intervention one day;
- every change may carry cost and time, and neither can be claimed or contested without a written record.

## Scaling the route to the impact

The site needs quick answers and the project needs control. We reconcile the two with three routes, chosen by the effect of the change, never by its urgency.

| Level | Typically | Who decides | What remains |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Management of change (MoC)** | Effect on safety, process, performance or scope; technical disagreement not settled at working level | Project management, with the owner | MoC file, risk assessment, formal approval |
| **Technical query (TQ)** | Routing, support, material, erection detail; deviation from an owner standard | Contractor's engineering; the owner for deviations from its standards | Numbered TQ, written reply, document revision |
| **Red-line mark-up** | Final position of an instrument, routing adjustment with no functional effect, measured dimensions | Contractor's supervisor, after engineering advice when in doubt | Stamped and signed master copy |

**Management of change** covers anything that touches safety, the process, performance or contract scope. It needs a risk assessment, often a partial HAZOP review following the IEC 61882 method, a decision by project management and the owner's agreement. It is slow by design. The trap to watch for is a significant change split into several technical queries to avoid it. The quality team should read open TQs by system, not one by one, to catch this.

**The technical query** is the default route for any change to an AFC document. The site asks in writing; engineering checks design integrity, function and compliance with codes and standards, and replies in writing. A reply that departs from an owner standard goes to the owner. On the gas project mentioned above, engineering also kept a design change register: origin of the request, source document, summary, technical evaluation, the contractor's decision where there was a commercial impact, and the documents affected. Technical evaluation and commercial decision sat in separate columns, so each solution was judged on its merits before anyone discussed its cost.

**The red-line mark-up** handles minor adjustments, where a trip through engineering would be out of proportion. The supervisor decides with the subcontractor and marks the change in red on a master copy, with a stamp, a date and a signature. It is the simplest route and the one most often neglected. On one large project the main contractor had to send a formal instruction to all its subcontractors, midway through construction, reminding them to keep one master copy per work package in a known place, update it as the work progressed and hand it over complete before mechanical completion. By then, part of the information was already lost.

At the end of the work, mark-ups and TQ replies are incorporated into the documents by their originator or by engineering. The result is the as-built set, which the operator receives at handover.

## What supervisors must not treat as minor

Most uncontrolled changes are made in good faith by supervisors who did not see the engineering question behind a field adjustment. We give site teams a short list of subjects that always go at least to a technical query:

- anything that adds or removes elbows, high points or low points on a process line (flexibility, vents, drains);
- support type or position on lines subject to thermal movement or vibration;
- separation distances between process, electrical and instrument routes;
- access for operation and maintenance to valves, instruments and isolation points;
- anything inside or at the boundary of a hazardous area.

## Document control comes first

No change route works if the crew is building from the wrong revision. On the projects where change control held, document control rested on a few habits:

- a register tracking, package by package (civil, racks and piping, electrical and instrumentation, mechanical, skids, commissioning preparation), the status of each document up to AFC issue;
- distribution to subcontractors by transmittal through the project document platform, with acknowledgement of receipt;
- reports on outstanding documents, weekly during engineering and monthly afterwards;
- field checks by supervisors and the quality team on which revision the erection crew is actually using.

ISO 10007 calls this configuration management: identify the reference, control changes to it, record the actual status and verify that the physical item and its documentation agree. ISO 9001 sets the principle in its requirements on documented information and control of changes.

## Mechanical completion is where the bill arrives

At mechanical completion of each subsystem, the installation is compared with the latest AFC revision, red-lines included, and the owner signs the check sheets. At provisional acceptance, the whole is checked against the latest revision, or the as-built set where available. Untracked changes surface here, when the people who made them may have left the site.

New requirements can also appear at this stage. On one project the owner asked, midway through mechanical completion, for every drawing in a subsystem to be reissued at its latest revision, not only the boundary drawings, which the procedure did not foresee. The request had its logic, but it disrupted the first weeks of the phase. How mark-ups are treated in completion dossiers should be agreed with the owner before the first dossier is compiled.

## The contractual record

Under FIDIC conditions, a change instructed or accepted by the employer is a *variation*, which may entitle the contractor to an adjustment of price and time. For other events, a contractor that considers itself entitled to additional payment or time must give notice within a strict period, failing which the claim is barred: in both the 1999 and 2017 editions, 28 days from when it became aware, or should have become aware, of the event.

A TQ register with dates and replies is therefore also the contract file: who asked for what, when, on what basis, and with what answer. Verbal field decisions feed claims in both directions that nobody can settle on the documents.

## Settings for the next project

- No change without a TQ or a signed mark-up; a safety emergency is regularised the same day.
- Route by impact. A process change made in a hurry still goes through MoC.
- One master copy per package, a named holder, and a check during every site audit.
- Mark-ups incorporated as the work progresses, not rebuilt after demobilisation.
- Every change linked to its contractual effect and, where needed, notified within the time limit.
- Three indicators reviewed monthly: open TQs and their age, mark-ups not yet incorporated, deviations found during completion walkdowns. When the second figure keeps rising during construction, expect acceptance difficulties a few months later.

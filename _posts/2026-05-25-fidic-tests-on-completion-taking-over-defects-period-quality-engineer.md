The contractor's letter was two paragraphs long. The Defects Notification Period would expire on a stated date; the contractor expected the Performance Certificate within 28 days and the release of its security. On the employer's desk sat several open defect notifications. One concerned a leaking reboiler in a process unit. The contractor said the leak came from operating conditions outside the design envelope. The operator said it was a fabrication defect. Neither side had a joint inspection report written at the time of the leak, nor the operating data for the days before it. The argument about who should pay could not be settled from the file, so it outlasted the defects period.

The evidence that settles such cases is created, or not, long before the final month. This article therefore reads Clauses 9, 10 and 11 of the Red Book 2017 backwards, from the Performance Certificate to the quality plan.

## The last 28 days: what the Performance Certificate requires

Under Sub-Clause 11.9, the Engineer issues the Performance Certificate within **28 days** after the latest expiry date of the Defects Notification Periods, or as soon thereafter as the contractor has supplied all the Contractor's Documents and completed and tested all the works, including remedying any defects. If the Engineer fails to issue it, it is deemed issued 28 days after the date on which it should have been issued.

Only the Performance Certificate constitutes **acceptance of the works**. After it, each party remains liable for obligations still unperformed. For plant, that liability is limited to two years after the end of the Defects Notification Period, except in cases of fraud, gross negligence, deliberate default or reckless misconduct, or where the law provides otherwise.

A contractor that asks for the certificate on a fixed date is entitled to ask. An employer who wants to hold it back must show what remains to be done, and that the open defects are the contractor's to remedy.

## During the defects period: the cause decides who pays

### Notification and joint inspection

Sub-Clause 11.1 requires the contractor to complete the outstanding work listed in the Taking-Over Certificate within the time stated or instructed, and to remedy defects or damage notified before the period expires. When a defect appears, the parties **inspect it jointly**, the contractor proposes remedial work, and the Sub-Clause 7.5 procedure applies.

The Defects Notification Period is stated in the Contract Data, and is **one year** if nothing is stated. It runs from the Date of Completion of the works, Section or part.

### Attribution

Under Sub-Clause 11.2, remedial work is at the contractor's cost to the extent it results from the contractor's design, from plant, materials or workmanship not in accordance with the contract, from improper operation or maintenance attributable to the contractor (including deficient as-built records, manuals or training), or from any other breach. If the contractor considers that the cause lies elsewhere, it gives notice, and the Engineer agrees or determines the cause. Work found not to be attributable to the contractor is valued as a variation.

On a process plant, the debate almost always turns on the same alternative: design or fabrication on one side, operation on the other. In the reboiler case, the useful evidence would have been simple to collect on the day of the leak: photographs, the location and shape of the leak path, pressure and temperature trends for the previous weeks, the start-up and shutdown log, and a sample of the fluid. None of it was attached to the notification.

We therefore recommend a defect notification form that does more than describe the symptom. It records:

- the date and conditions of discovery, and who was present;
- the operating data for a defined period before the event (pressures, temperatures, flows, trips, start-ups);
- photographs and, where possible, retained samples or removed parts, labelled and stored;
- a first hypothesis of cause, marked as provisional;
- the date proposed for the joint inspection, and the signed record of that inspection.

A form like this costs an hour on the day of the event. Without it, attribution under 11.2 becomes a matter of opinion, and so does every remedy that depends on it: the extension of the period, by **no more than two years**, for a part that cannot be used because of a contractor defect (11.3, subject to the claims procedure), and the employer's right under 11.4 to fix a date for delayed remedial work, then carry it out at the contractor's cost, obtain a price reduction or, in serious cases, terminate for the affected part.

## At taking over: the list that follows you for a year

### Preconditions

Sub-Clause 10.1 sets conditions for taking over that go beyond physical completion:

- the works are completed in accordance with the contract, **including passing the Tests on Completion**, except for minor outstanding work and defects;
- where applicable, the Engineer has given, or is deemed to have given, a no-objection to the **as-built records**;
- where applicable, the same for the **operation and maintenance manuals**;
- where applicable, the contractor has carried out the required **training**;
- a Taking-Over Certificate has been issued or is deemed issued.

The documentary conditions were introduced in 2017. In our experience they are now the most frequent cause of late taking over. As-built records and manuals need Engineer review time, usually defined in the specification, so they must be submitted, reviewed and corrected before the application.

### Application and certificate

The contractor may apply by notice **no more than 14 days** before it expects the works to be complete. The Engineer then has **28 days** to issue the certificate, stating the Date of Completion and listing minor outstanding work and defects, or to reject the application with reasons. If the Engineer does neither and the conditions are met, the certificate is deemed issued and the works deemed complete on the fourteenth day after the application.

The phrase that matters most for quality limits the list: the outstanding work and defects must not **substantially affect the safe use** of the works for their intended purpose. Every item proposed for the list should be tested against that phrase. The logic is close to the punch list categories of industrial mechanical completion. An item that prevents safe use is a reason to reject the application and has no place on the list.

### What happens to the list afterwards

At a pumping station, the Taking-Over Certificate carried several hundred items. Most were cosmetic. A few were not, including a vibration reading on one pump close to the acceptance limit and missing as-built drawings for the electrical room. At the end of the defects period, dozens of items were still open. The site team had demobilised, the contractor's warranty team worked from its own list, the operator from another. The Performance Certificate came months late, the security was retained, and the pump with the borderline vibration had damaged its bearings in the meantime.

The fix is organisational. The list attached to the certificate becomes the single register for the defects period. The Engineer, the contractor and the operator share it, defects notified later are added to it, each line has an owner and a target date, and it is reviewed monthly with evidence of closure. An item close to an acceptance limit is flagged for re-measurement during the period, not left as a note.

### Sections and parts

Where the contract defines Sections, each is taken over separately, with its own Date of Completion and its own Defects Notification Period. Under Sub-Clause 10.2, the employer may also take over parts of the works. If it uses a part before taking over, other than as a temporary measure stated in the specification or agreed, that part is deemed taken over from the date of use. Public employers who open a road or a building "temporarily" start a defects period without noticing it.

## During the tests: criteria written in advance

### The programme

Under Sub-Clause 9.1, Tests on Completion follow the submission of the as-built records and the operation and maintenance manuals, where the contract requires them. At least **42 days** before the tests, the contractor submits a detailed test programme. The Engineer may give notice of non-compliance; the contractor revises within **14 days**. Silence for 14 days is a deemed no-objection, and the tests cannot start without one.

The contractor then gives at least **21 days'** notice of the date after which it will be ready for each test, and starts within 14 days of that date. Once the tests are passed, it submits a certified report. The Engineer has **14 days** to give notice that the results do not comply, failing which a no-objection is deemed given. When assessing results, the Engineer allows for the effect of any use of the works by the employer.

For the quality engineer, the review of the test programme is the point to check that every test has a written procedure, an acceptance criterion, calibrated instruments and a record format, and that the procedures cite the specification and standards actually in the contract. A criterion settled after the test produces a negotiation rather than a result. The test records are also the baseline that will be used, a year later, to judge whether a defect is due to fabrication or to operation.

### Delays, retests and failure

If the employer prevents the tests for more than 14 days, Sub-Clause 10.3 deems the works taken over on the date the tests would otherwise have been completed, and the tests are carried out later, before the end of the Defects Notification Period. If the contractor delays them, Sub-Clause 9.2 lets the Engineer require them within 21 days of a notice, and the employer may ultimately carry them out at the contractor's risk and cost.

After a failed retest (9.3), Sub-Clause 9.4 offers another retest, rejection of the works or of a Section, or a Taking-Over Certificate at the employer's request with a price reduction. That last option is the most used and the least documented; the reduction should rest on the measured shortfall, its effect on the employer and a stated valuation method.

## Before any of that: Sub-Clause 4.9

Sub-Clause 4.9, new in 2017, is where all the evidence above starts. The contractor submits a quality management system specific to the works within **28 days** of the Commencement Date. The Engineer may give notice of the extent to which it does not comply; if not, after 21 days the Engineer is deemed to have given a no-objection. The contractor audits the system **at least every six months** and reports within seven days. It also runs a **compliance verification system** that ends with a complete set of records proving that materials, plant and workmanship comply.

On one project, the compliance verification records of the first Section took four months to assemble after the works were finished: certificates filed by purchase order, laboratory results held by a subcontractor, inspection records without drawing revisions, hold points released by telephone. The remedy is to build the dossier as the works progress: an ITP for each work type that reconciles hold, witness and review points with inspection notices under Sub-Clause 7.3 and the Engineer's 72-hour notice to attend tests under 7.4; traceability on each record; non-conformities handled through 7.5; and a monthly report on dossier completeness.

## Tunisian public contracts: provisional and final acceptance

Tunisian public works contracts use *réception provisoire* and *réception définitive*, with a guarantee period between them. In a FIDIC-based public contract, the Taking-Over Certificate plays the first role and the Performance Certificate the second. The Particular Conditions should say so, align the Defects Notification Period with the guarantee period required by the applicable administrative conditions, and tie the release of the performance security and retention money to the Performance Certificate. Otherwise the guarantee release and the FIDIC certificates run on separate calendars, and a contractor can claim final acceptance under one while defects remain open under the other.

## The tools, in contract order

| Moment | Tool | Purpose |
|---|---|---|
| Six months before tests | Test matrix | Every test in the specification, with procedure, criterion, instruments, responsibility |
| 42 days before tests | Test programme and procedures | Contractual submission under 9.1 |
| Before applying for taking over | Documentation tracker | As-built records, manuals, training records, compliance verification dossier |
| At taking over | Single outstanding work and defects list | One list, sorted against the safe-use test, with owners and target dates |
| During the defects period | Defects register and notification form | Notification, operating data, joint inspection, cause, remedy, retest, closure |
| Three months before expiry | Close-out review | Open items, extension under 11.3 if justified, Contractor's Documents complete |

Scheduled three months before expiry, the close-out review leaves time to agree attribution on the remaining defects, to give the notices an extension under 11.3 requires, and to answer a request for the Performance Certificate with a dated list of what remains.

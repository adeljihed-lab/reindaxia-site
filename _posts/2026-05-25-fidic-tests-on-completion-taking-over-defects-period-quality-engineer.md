The contractor's letter was two paragraphs long: the Defects Notification Period would expire on a stated date, and the contractor expected the Performance Certificate within 28 days and the release of its security. On the employer's desk sat several open defect notifications. One concerned a centrifugal pump in a water treatment plant whose mechanical seal had failed twice. The contractor said the pump had been run dry during a shutdown; the operator said the seal had been wrongly assembled at the factory. Neither side had a joint inspection report written when the first seal failed, nor the operating trends for the days before it. The question of who should pay could not be settled from the records, so it outlasted the defects period.

The evidence that settles such questions is created, or not, long before the final month. This article therefore reads Clauses 9, 10 and 11 of the Red Book 2017 backwards, from the Performance Certificate to the quality plan.

## The last 28 days: what the Performance Certificate requires

Under Sub-Clause 11.9, the Engineer issues the Performance Certificate within **28 days** after the latest expiry date of the Defects Notification Periods, or as soon thereafter as the contractor has supplied all the Contractor's Documents and completed and tested all the works, including remedying any defects. If the Engineer fails to issue it, it is deemed issued 28 days late.

Only the Performance Certificate constitutes **acceptance of the works**. After it, each party remains liable for obligations still unperformed; for plant, that liability is limited to two years after the end of the Defects Notification Period, save for fraud, gross negligence, deliberate default or reckless misconduct, or where the law provides otherwise.

An employer who wants to hold the certificate back must show what remains to be done, and that the open defects are the contractor's to remedy.

## During the defects period: the cause decides who pays

### Notification and joint inspection

Sub-Clause 11.1 requires the contractor to complete the outstanding work listed in the Taking-Over Certificate and to remedy defects notified before the period expires. When a defect appears, the parties **inspect it jointly**, the contractor proposes remedial work, and the Sub-Clause 7.5 procedure applies. The period is stated in the Contract Data (**one year** by default) and runs from the Date of Completion of the works, Section or part.

### Attribution

Under Sub-Clause 11.2, remedial work is at the contractor's cost to the extent it results from the contractor's design, from plant, materials or workmanship not in accordance with the contract, from improper operation or maintenance attributable to the contractor (including deficient as-built records, manuals or training), or from any other breach. If the contractor considers that the cause lies elsewhere, it gives notice, and the Engineer agrees or determines the cause. Work not attributable to the contractor is valued as a variation.

On a process plant, the debate almost always turns on the same alternative: design or fabrication on one side, operation on the other. For the pump seal, the useful evidence would have been simple to collect on the day of the failure: photographs of the seal faces, suction pressure and flow trends for the previous weeks, the start-up and shutdown log, and the removed seal, bagged and labelled. None of it was attached to the notification.

We therefore recommend a defect notification form that does more than describe the symptom. It records:

- the date and conditions of discovery, and who was present;
- the operating data for a defined period before the event (pressures, temperatures, flows, trips, start-ups);
- photographs and, where possible, retained samples or removed parts, labelled and stored;
- a first hypothesis of cause, marked as provisional;
- the date proposed for the joint inspection, and the signed record of that inspection.

A form like this costs an hour on the day of the event. Without it, attribution under 11.2 becomes a matter of opinion, and so does every remedy that depends on it: the extension of the period, by **no more than two years**, for a part that cannot be used because of a contractor defect (11.3), and the employer's rights under 11.4 when remedial work is delayed.

## At taking over: the list that follows you for a year

### Preconditions

Sub-Clause 10.1 sets conditions for taking over that go beyond physical completion: the works completed in accordance with the contract, **including passing the Tests on Completion**, except for minor outstanding work and defects; where applicable, the Engineer's no-objection (given or deemed) to the **as-built records** and to the **operation and maintenance manuals**, and the required **training** carried out; and a Taking-Over Certificate issued or deemed issued.

The documentary conditions were introduced in 2017, and in our experience they are now the most frequent cause of late taking over. On a road project, the works were physically complete for weeks while the as-built drawings went back and forth with the Engineer; the contractor had planned no review time at all. As-built records and manuals must be submitted, reviewed and corrected before the application.

### Application and certificate

The contractor may apply by notice **no more than 14 days** before it expects the works to be complete. The Engineer then has **28 days** to issue the certificate, stating the Date of Completion and listing minor outstanding work and defects, or to reject the application with reasons. If the Engineer does neither and the conditions are met, the certificate is deemed issued on the fourteenth day after the application.

The phrase that matters most for quality limits the list: the outstanding work and defects must not **substantially affect the safe use** of the works for their intended purpose. An item that prevents safe use is a reason to reject the application, and has no place on the list.

### What happens to the list afterwards

At a pumping station, the Taking-Over Certificate carried a long list of items, most of them cosmetic. A few were not, including a vibration reading on one pump close to the acceptance limit and missing as-built drawings for the electrical room. At the end of the defects period, dozens of items were still open: the contractor's warranty team worked from its own list, the operator from another. The Performance Certificate came months late, and the pump with the borderline vibration had damaged its bearings in the meantime.

The fix is organisational. The list attached to the certificate becomes the single register for the defects period, shared by the Engineer, the contractor and the operator, with an owner and a target date on each line and a monthly review with evidence of closure. An item close to an acceptance limit is flagged for re-measurement during the period, not left as a note.

### Sections and parts

Where the contract defines Sections, each is taken over separately, with its own Defects Notification Period. Under Sub-Clause 10.2, a part of the works that the employer uses before taking over, other than as an agreed temporary measure, is deemed taken over from the date of use. On a school building, the employer moved furniture in before the Tests on Completion; the contractor later argued, with some success, that the defects period had started that day.

## During the tests: criteria written in advance

### The programme

Under Sub-Clause 9.1, Tests on Completion follow the submission of the as-built records and the operation and maintenance manuals, where the contract requires them. At least **42 days** before the tests, the contractor submits a detailed test programme; the Engineer may give notice of non-compliance, the contractor revises within **14 days**, and silence for 14 days is a deemed no-objection, without which the tests cannot start.

The contractor then gives at least **21 days'** notice of the date after which it will be ready for each test, and starts within 14 days of that date. Once the tests are passed, it submits a certified report; the Engineer has **14 days** to give notice that the results do not comply, failing which a no-objection is deemed given.

For the quality engineer, the review of the test programme is the point to check that every test has a written procedure, an acceptance criterion, calibrated instruments and a record format, and that the procedures cite the specification and standards actually in the contract. At the commissioning of a compressor station, the performance test procedure gave no tolerance on the guaranteed flow; the measured value fell a little short, and three weeks went into arguing whether the shortfall was within measurement uncertainty. A criterion settled after the test produces a negotiation rather than a result. The test records are also the baseline used, a year later, to judge whether a defect is due to fabrication or to operation.

The programme review is also where the means of testing are checked, since a test that cannot start on the notified date is charged to whoever failed to provide for it. On a process plant, the test water and nitrogen had been specified at design stage without asking who would deliver them, in what quantity and when; the first hydrotests waited for tankers, and the water finally used left corrosion in lines that then had to be re-flushed. The programme should state, for each test, the medium and its quality, the quantities, the source and the date it must be on site, reconciled with the construction schedule. The same goes for the instruments that prove the acceptance criteria: on one commissioning, the only calibrated pressure recorder on site broke down during a schedule-critical test pack and no spare existed, so the results of a leak test could not be recorded; the gauges used for cleanliness and tightness checks also turned out to be outside their calibration dates. A list of test instruments with calibration status and a spare for each critical item belongs in the programme submission, as does the list of vendor representatives the tests require, with dates.

### Delays, retests and failure

If the employer prevents the tests for more than 14 days, Sub-Clause 10.3 deems the works taken over on the date the tests would otherwise have been completed, and the tests follow during the Defects Notification Period. If the contractor delays them, Sub-Clause 9.2 lets the Engineer require them within 21 days of a notice, and the employer may ultimately carry them out at the contractor's risk and cost.

After a failed retest (9.3), Sub-Clause 9.4 offers another retest, rejection of the works or of a Section, or a Taking-Over Certificate at the employer's request with a price reduction. That last option is the most used and the least documented; the reduction should rest on the measured shortfall, its effect on the employer and a stated valuation method.

## Before any of that: Sub-Clause 4.9

Sub-Clause 4.9, new in 2017, is where all the evidence above starts. The contractor submits a quality management system specific to the works within **28 days** of the Commencement Date; the Engineer may give notice of non-compliance, and after 21 days without one is deemed to have given a no-objection. The contractor audits the system **at least every six months** and reports within seven days. It also runs a **compliance verification system** that ends with a complete set of records proving that materials, plant and workmanship comply.

On an industrial building, the compliance verification records of the first Section took months to assemble after the works were finished: certificates filed by purchase order, laboratory results held by a subcontractor, hold points released by telephone. The remedy is to build the records as the works progress: an ITP for each work type that reconciles hold, witness and review points with inspection notices under Sub-Clause 7.3 and the Engineer's 72-hour notice under 7.4, traceability on each record, non-conformities handled through 7.5, and a monthly report on the completeness of the records.

## Tunisian public contracts: provisional and final acceptance

Tunisian public works contracts use *réception provisoire* and *réception définitive*, with a guarantee period between them. In a FIDIC-based public contract, the Taking-Over Certificate plays the first role and the Performance Certificate the second. The Particular Conditions should say so, align the Defects Notification Period with the guarantee period of the applicable administrative conditions, and tie the release of the performance security and retention money to the Performance Certificate. Otherwise a contractor can claim final acceptance under one calendar while defects remain open under the other.

## The tools, in contract order

| Moment | Tool | Purpose |
|---|---|---|
| Six months before tests | Test matrix | Every test in the specification, with procedure, criterion, instruments, test media, vendor attendance, responsibility |
| 42 days before tests | Test programme and procedures | Contractual submission under 9.1 |
| Before applying for taking over | Documentation tracker | As-built records, manuals, training records, compliance verification records |
| At taking over | Single outstanding work and defects list | One list, sorted against the safe-use test, with owners and target dates |
| During the defects period | Defects register and notification form | Notification, operating data, joint inspection, cause, remedy, retest, closure |
| Three months before expiry | Close-out review | Open items, extension under 11.3 if justified, Contractor's Documents complete |

Scheduled three months before expiry, the close-out review leaves time to agree attribution on the remaining defects, to give the notices an extension under 11.3 requires, and to answer a letter like the one on the employer's desk with a dated list of what remains.
